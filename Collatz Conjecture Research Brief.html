The Collatz Conjecture

Take any positive integer. Halve it if even, triple it and add 1 if odd, and repeat. Does every starting number eventually reach 1? After nearly ninety years, enormous computation, and one of the strongest “almost all” theorems in modern number theory — nobody knows. This brief gives the exact statements, the latest verified bound, and the honest boundary between proof, heuristic, and hope.

Section 1

Statement and definitions

Three maps are used interchangeably in the literature. They have the same convergence behaviour but different step counts — always check which one a source uses.

The Collatz map:

$$C(n)=\begin{cases} n/2, & n\equiv 0 \pmod 2,\\ 3n+1, & n\equiv 1 \pmod 2,\end{cases}\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}.$$

The accelerated (\(3x+1\)) map \(T\) folds in the forced halving after an odd step — this is the \(T\) of Lagarias's surveys and of Bařina's project:

$$T(n)=\begin{cases} n/2, & n\equiv 0 \pmod 2,\\ (3n+1)/2, & n\equiv 1 \pmod 2.\end{cases}$$

The Syracuse map acts only on odd integers, dividing out all factors of 2, where \(\nu_2(m)\) is the exponent of 2 in \(m\). Tao's paper is phrased largely through this map:

$$\mathrm{Syr}(n)=\frac{3n+1}{2^{\nu_2(3n+1)}},\qquad n\ \text{odd}.$$
The Collatz conjecture CONJECTURAL
For every \(n\ge 1\) there exists \(k\ge 0\) with \(C^k(n)=1\); thereafter the orbit cycles \(1\to4\to2\to1\). Equivalently, \(\mathrm{Col}_{\min}(N):=\inf_{k\ge0} C^k(N) = 1\) for all \(N\). — Conjecture 1.1 in Tao (2022). Cambridge page

A counterexample would have to be of exactly one of two types PROVEN (elementary): (i) a divergent orbit — unbounded, never repeating; or (ii) an orbit entering a nontrivial cycle \(\ne (1,4,2)\). A bounded non-repeating orbit of a deterministic map on \(\mathbb{Z}\) is impossible, which is why these are the only two failure modes. Source: Lagarias overview, arXiv:2111.02635.

Trajectory, stopping times, records

The trajectory (orbit) of \(n\) is \(n, C(n), C^2(n), \dots\) Its values are called hailstone numbers — they rise and fall like hailstones in a cloud — or “wondrous numbers” in older popular writing.

$$\sigma(n)=\min\{k\ge 1 : T^k(n) < n\},\qquad \sigma_\infty(n)=\min\{k\ge 0 : T^k(n)=1\},$$

with value \(\infty\) if the set is empty. \(\sigma\) is the stopping time (first drop below the start); \(\sigma_\infty\) is the total stopping time (first arrival at 1). The conjecture is equivalent to: every \(n\ge2\) has finite \(\sigma(n)\) — if every number drops below itself, induction on \(n\) finishes the proof.

Why conventions matter — \(n=27\). Under \(C\) it takes 111 steps to reach 1 and peaks at 9232; under \(T\) it takes 70 steps. Both numbers appear in the literature as “the” stopping time of 27; neither is wrong. Verify it yourself in the explorer above.

Let \(t(n)=\max_{k\ge0} T^k(n)\) be the maximum excursion. Then \(n\) is a path record if \(t(m)<t(n)\) for all \(m<n\), and a total-stopping-time (delay) record-setter if \(\sigma_\infty(m)<\sigma_\infty(n)\) for all \(m<n\). Delay record-setters under \(C\) begin \(1,2,3,6,7,9,18,25,27,54,73,97,129,171,231,313,327,649,703,871,1161,2223,\dots\) (OEIS A006877); path record-setters begin \(1,2,3,7,15,27,255,447,639,703,1819,4255,\dots\) (OEIS A006884). Source: Bařina path-records page.

Name variants PROVEN as historical terminology

  • Syracuse problem — name proposed by Helmut Hasse during a visit to Syracuse University in the 1950s.
  • Kakutani's problem — Shizuo Kakutani circulated it c. 1960, at Yale and Chicago.
  • Ulam's problem / Ulam conjecture — Stanisław Ulam circulated it at Los Alamos.
  • Also: Hasse's algorithm, Thwaites's conjecture (Bryan Thwaites claimed independent discovery in 1952), and simply the \(3x+1\) / \(3n+1\) problem.

Source: Lagarias (1985), HTML introduction; Lagarias overview, arXiv:2111.02635.

Section 2

History

The origin is genuinely obscure because the problem circulated orally for ~20 years before print. What follows is what Lagarias's archival work supports — with the popular simplification flagged.

1932 — the documented seed. PROVEN Lothar Collatz's notebook dated 1 July 1932 contains a related problem — the cycle structure of a permutation of \(\mathbb{N}\) built from a \(3x\)-type function — not the \(3x+1\) iteration in modern form. The common statement “Collatz proposed the conjecture in 1937, two years after his 1935 doctorate” is the standard attribution in reference works; Lagarias, who corresponded with Collatz, presents the 1932 notebook as the documented origin and the exact origin of the \(3x+1\) form as obscure. Both views are given here, as the sources require.
1950. Collatz circulated iteration problems at the International Congress of Mathematicians, Cambridge, Massachusetts. PROVEN per Lagarias.
1952. Thwaites independently discovered the problem; Collatz discussed it with Hasse (hence “Hasse's algorithm”). Hasse proposed the name “Syracuse problem” in the 1950s.
c. 1960. Kakutani heard it and circulated it at Yale — “for about a month everybody at Yale worked on it, with no result” — and Chicago, where the joke arose that it was “part of a conspiracy to slow down mathematical research in the U.S.” Ulam circulated it at Los Alamos. (Reported quotation, Lagarias 1985.)
1963. First publication Lagarias can trace concerning an iteration problem of this general sort: a problem proposed by Klamkin, about Collatz's original permutation function — not yet the \(3x+1\) function itself.
1972. Conway, “Unpredictable iterations” — generalized Collatz functions are undecidable (§7). This motivated / preceded the first research papers on the \(3x+1\) function itself. PROVEN
1976–77. First research publications on the \(3x+1\) function itself: Terras (1976), Everett (1977), Davidson (1977). The density theorem of §4.
1977. Steiner: no nontrivial 1-cycle (§4).
1985. Lagarias, “The \(3x+1\) problem and its generalizations”, Amer. Math. Monthly 92, 3–23 — the survey that organized the field. DOI 10.1080/00029890.1985.11971528.
1992. Lagarias–Weiss stochastic models (§6).
1994. Korec improves the density exponent to \(\theta>\log 3/\log 4\) (§4).
2003. Krasikov–Lagarias \(x^{0.84}\) counting bound (§4).
2005. Simons–de Weger: no \(m\)-cycles for \(m\le 68\) (§4).
2010. Lagarias (ed.), The Ultimate Challenge: The \(3x+1\) Problem, AMS — collected surveys, reprints, bibliography. Publisher page.
2019 / 2022. Tao's “almost bounded values” theorem — arXiv September 2019, published Forum Math. Pi 2022 (§4).
2020–2026. Bařina's distributed verification: \(2^{68}\) (2020) → \(2^{71}\) published (2025) → \(2075\times2^{60}\) live (2026) (§3). COMPUTATIONAL

Prizes (colour, not progress): Coxeter \$50 (1970), Erdős \$500, Thwaites £1,000 are reported by Lagarias (1985). Erdős's remark “Mathematics may not be ready for such problems” is widely attributed — treat it as a reported quotation, not a theorem.

How verification works COMPUTATIONAL

Blocks of integers are tested sequentially on thousands of parallel workers on European supercomputers, with a \(3^k\) sieve (implemented for \(k=2\)) discarding residue classes already known to descend, and GPU acceleration totalling \(1335\times\) over the initial CPU algorithm (2025 paper). It suffices to test odd starts / representatives: if every \(n\) in a block drops below its starting value to a number already verified, induction extends verification.

Section 4

Proven partial results

All statements are PROVEN unless tagged otherwise. “Almost all” must always be read with its density specified — natural density and logarithmic density are different notions, and Tao's improvement trades one for the other.

(a) Density / descent results

Terras (1976) — Everett (1977). The set \(\{n : \sigma(n) < \infty\}\) has natural density 1; more precisely Terras showed the set of \(n\) with (coefficient) stopping time \(\le k\) has a limiting asymptotic density \(F(k)\), and \(F(k)\to1\) as \(k\to\infty\). In Tao's notation: \(\mathrm{Col}_{\min}(N) < N\) for almost all \(N\) (natural density).
R. Terras, Acta Arith. 30 (1976), 241–252, DOI 10.4064/aa-30-3-241-252 · C. J. Everett, Adv. Math. 25 (1977), 42–45. Correction: Everett's paper is 1977, not 1976 — a common miscitation. Terras journal listing

Allouche (1979). \(\mathrm{Col}_{\min}(N) < N^{\theta}\) for almost all \(N\) (natural density), for any fixed \(\theta \approx 0.869\) (as stated in Tao).

Korec (1994). For any fixed \(\theta > \dfrac{\log 3}{\log 4} \approx 0.7924\), \(\mathrm{Col}_{\min}(N) \le N^{\theta}\) for almost all \(N\) (natural density). I. Korec, Math. Slovaca 44 (1994), 85–89.

Tao (2019 / 2022) — the strongest “almost all” theorem. Theorem 1.3: let \(f:\mathbb{N}+1\to\mathbb{R}\) be any function with \(\lim_{N\to\infty} f(N)=+\infty\). Then $$\mathrm{Col}_{\min}(N) < f(N)\quad\text{for almost all } N\ \text{(in the sense of logarithmic density).}$$ E.g. \(\mathrm{Col}_{\min}(N) < \log\log\log\log N\) for almost all \(N\). Equivalent form (Remark 1.4): for every \(\delta>0\) there is \(C_\delta\) with \(\mathrm{Col}_{\min}(N)\le C_\delta\) on a set of lower logarithmic density \(\ge 1-\delta\). It does not give a constant \(C_0\) on a density-1 set, and says nothing about any individual \(N\).
T. Tao, Forum Math. Pi 10 (2022), e12, DOI 10.1017/fmp.2022.8; arXiv:1909.03562. Proof idea in one line: work with Syracuse orbits, show the \(\nu_2\)-valuation vector behaves like i.i.d. geometric variables, prove fine-scale 3-adic equidistribution / stabilisation of a first-passage distribution, and iterate across scales.

Inselmann (2024, preprint). Extends the Terras–Everett approximation: for almost all \(m\) (natural density), \(T^k(m)\) tracks \((\sqrt3/2)^k m^{1\pm\varepsilon}\) simultaneously for \(0\le k\le \alpha\log m\) with maximal \(\alpha = 2(\log\frac43)^{-1}\approx6.952\) (previously \(\alpha=(\log2)^{-1}\)); consequently \(T_{\min}(m)\le m^{\varepsilon}\) for almost all \(m\). Status: arXiv preprint, not marked peer-reviewed in the version read — treat as proven as claimed in preprint, unrefereed. arXiv:2402.03276

(b) Counting bounds — how many \(n\le x\) are proved to reach 1

Let \(\pi_a(x)=\#\{n\le x : a \text{ occurs in the } T\text{-orbit of } n\}\) (\(a\not\equiv0\pmod3\)).

  • Applegate–Lagarias (1995), tree-search and Krasikov-inequality methods: \(\pi_a(x) \gg x^{0.809}\) for large \(x\). Math. Comp. 64 (1995), 411–426 and 427–438.
  • Krasikov–Lagarias (2003) — best published exponent: for each fixed \(a\not\equiv0\pmod3\), $$\pi_a(x) \gg x^{0.84}\qquad\text{for all sufficiently large } x,$$ in particular \(\#\{N\le x : \mathrm{Col}_{\min}(N)=1\} \gg x^{0.84}\). Method: systems of difference inequalities over residue classes mod \(3^k\), solved as a linear program. Acta Arith. 109 (2003), 237–258, DOI 10.4064/aa109-3-4. Note: \(x^{0.84}\) is a vanishing proportion of \([1,x]\) — far weaker than density 1, but unconditional, and it counts numbers proved to reach 1 itself, not merely to drop.

(c) Cycles — nonexistence of small cycles

Terminology: an \(m\)-cycle is a cycle partitionable into \(m\) blocks (“circuits”), each an increasing run of odd iterates followed by a decreasing run of even iterates; \(m\) is the number of local minima. This \(m\) is not the cycle length.

  • Steiner (1977). No nontrivial 1-cycle exists: the only 1-cycle is the trivial \((1,2)\) (for \(T\)). Uses Baker-type bounds on linear forms in logarithms. The statement is confirmed in multiple secondary sources; the primary proceedings paper was not opened in the underlying research — see caveats.
  • Simons (2004/2005). No nontrivial 2-cycle, by Steiner's method.
  • Simons–de Weger (2005), as published. No nontrivial \(m\)-cycle for \(m\le 68\). Acta Arith. 117 (2005), 51–70. Full text (PDF)
  • Hercher (2023) — current published record. No \(m\)-cycle with \(m\le 91\), i.e. any nontrivial cycle has \(m\ge 92\). J. Integer Seq. 26 (2023), Article 23.3.5; arXiv:2201.00406 · journal PDF
  • Eliahou (1993). Using continued-fraction convergents of \(\log_2 3\) plus the then-current verification bound, any nontrivial cycle has total length at least \(17{,}087{,}915\); in fact its length lies in an explicit sparse semigroup generated by convergent denominators. Discrete Math. 118 (1993), 45–56, DOI 10.1016/0012-365X(93)90052-U.

An apparent disagreement, resolved. Hercher's abstract states “Simons and de Weger proved \(m\ge76\)” — that is their method updated with the larger verification bound available by 2022, not the \(m\le68\) headline of the 2005 paper; with still newer bounds the same method gives \(m\ge83\). Three statements about (paper, method + old data, method + new data) — not a contradiction. Hercher also shows verification up to \(1536\times2^{60}=3\times2^{69}\) would suffice (by his method) to force \(\ge 1.375\times10^{11}\) odd members in any nontrivial cycle — Bařina's subsequent \(2^{71}\) verification exceeds \(3\times2^{69}\), and Bařina (2025) quotes the resulting total-length bound 355,504,839,929 (§3). The two numbers count different things (odd members vs total length) and must not be interchanged.

(d) Bounds on stopping time as a function of \(n\)

  • Trivial lower bound PROVEN: \(\sigma_\infty(n) \ge \log n / \log 2\) for the \(T\) map (each step at most halves), with equality for \(n=2^k\).
  • Applegate–Lagarias (2003). There are infinitely many \(n\) with finite total stopping time (\(T\) map) satisfying $$\sigma_\infty(n) > 6.14316\,\log n \qquad(\log = \text{natural log}),$$ proved by a computer-intensive tree search to depth 60 (certificate of ~\(3.5\times10^8\) trees); also \(\ge c x^{1/60}\) integers \(\le x\) have \(\sigma_\infty(n) > 5.9\log n\). Math. Comp. 72 (2003), 1035–1049; arXiv:math/0103054. The heuristic expectation is \(6.95212\log n\) for typical \(n\) (§5–§6), so 6.14316 is a rigorous lower bound for infinitely many \(n\), below the typical value — a different quantifier, not a conflict.
  • Amano (2021, preprint) reports extending that computation to reach the heuristic constant \(\gamma_0\approx6.9521\) for infinitely many \(n\). Status: preprint only — treat as claimed, unrefereed.
  • No upper bound: PROVEN as a statement about current knowledge — there is no known function \(F\) with \(\sigma_\infty(n)\le F(n)\) for all \(n\). Such a bound would essentially resolve the conjecture. Tao / Inselmann give upper bounds on descent for almost all \(n\), not on \(\sigma_\infty\) for all \(n\).

Section 5

Key equations and inequalities

Parity-vector / affine formula PROVEN

Let \(v_k(n)=(n\bmod 2,\; T(n)\bmod 2,\dots, T^{k-1}(n)\bmod 2)\) be the parity vector of length \(k\), and \(o_k\) the number of odd steps among the first \(k\) \(T\)-steps. Then there is an integer \(r_k\ge0\), depending only on the parity vector, with

$$T^{k}(n)=\frac{3^{o_k}\,n + r_k}{2^{k}}.$$

\(n\bmod 2^k\) determines \(v_k(n)\), and \(v_k\) is a bijection between \(\mathbb{Z}/2^k\mathbb{Z}\) and \(\{0,1\}^k\): every finite parity pattern occurs, for exactly one residue class mod \(2^k\). This is the root of both the heuristic (§6) and the hardness (§7): the map imposes no finite-length restriction on parity patterns.

Drift inequality

\(T^k(n) < n\) iff (ignoring \(r_k\)) \(3^{o_k} < 2^k\), i.e.

$$\frac{o_k}{k} < \frac{\log 2}{\log 3} \approx 0.6309.$$

Terras's coefficient stopping time is the first \(k\) with \(3^{o_k}/2^k < 1\); whether coefficient descent always implies actual descent is Terras's Coefficient Stopping Time Conjecture — CONJECTURAL, still open in general. Do not present it as proved.

2-adic formulation and conjugacy PROVEN

Extend \(T\) to the 2-adic integers \(\mathbb{Z}_2\) by the same formula. Let \(S:\mathbb{Z}_2\to\mathbb{Z}_2\), \(S(x)=x/2\) if \(x\) even, \((x-1)/2\) if \(x\) odd — the shift that deletes the first binary digit. Then \(T\) and \(S\) are conjugate: there is a homeomorphism \(\Phi:\mathbb{Z}_2\to\mathbb{Z}_2\) with

$$\Phi\circ S\circ\Phi^{-1} = T,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \Phi\circ S = T\circ\Phi.$$

The inverse is the parity-vector map:

$$\Phi^{-1}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty} \bigl(T^{i}(x)\bmod 2\bigr)\,2^{i}\ \in\mathbb{Z}_2,$$

i.e. \(\Phi^{-1}(x)\) is the 2-adic integer whose binary digits are the successive parities of the \(T\)-orbit of \(x\). Bernstein gave an explicit (non-iterative) formula for \(\Phi\). \(T\) preserves 2-adic Haar measure and is ergodic / strongly mixing with respect to it (Matthews–Watts, as stated in Lagarias). Bernstein & Lagarias, Canad. J. Math. 48 (1996), DOI 10.4153/CJM-1996-060-x. In this language the conjecture becomes a statement about orbits of rational (indeed integer) points inside a system whose typical (Haar-a.e.) behaviour is completely understood — and integers are a Haar-null set. That gap is the problem.

Cycle equations PROVEN

If \(x\) lies on a \(T\)-cycle of total length \(N=k+\ell\) with \(k\) odd steps, the affine formula with \(T^N(x)=x\) gives

$$x\,\bigl(2^{N}-3^{k}\bigr) = r_N,\qquad\text{hence}\qquad 2^{N} > 3^{k},\ \ \frac{N}{k} > \log_2 3 \approx 1.58496.$$

For a cycle under \(C\) with odd elements \(n_1,\dots,n_k\), the product form telescopes to

$$\frac{2^{N}}{3^{k}}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{3n_i}\Bigr),\qquad\text{so}\qquad 1 < \frac{2^{N}}{3^{k}} < \Bigl(1+\frac{1}{3\,n_{\min}}\Bigr)^{k},$$ $$0 < N\log 2 - k\log 3 < \frac{k}{3\,n_{\min}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 0 < \frac{N}{k} - \log_2 3 < \frac{1}{3\,n_{\min}\log 2}.$$

Thus \(N/k\) must be an extraordinarily good rational approximation to the irrational \(\log_2 3\), with quality forced by \(n_{\min}\) exceeding the verification bound. By continued-fraction theory \(N/k\) must be a convergent / semiconvergent of \(\log_2 3\) — this is the Eliahou / Simons–de Weger / Hercher engine (§4c). Baker's theory of linear forms in logarithms bounds \(k\) in terms of \(m\) for \(m\)-cycles.

Expected total stopping time HEURISTIC

Model successive \(T\)-steps as independent fair coin flips: multiply by \(3/2\) (odd, prob. \(1/2\)) or \(1/2\) (even, prob. \(1/2\)). Then

$$\mathbb{E}[\Delta\log n] = \tfrac12\log\tfrac32 + \tfrac12\log\tfrac12 = \tfrac12\log\tfrac34 < 0,$$

so \(\log n\) is a random walk with negative drift \(\tfrac12\log(4/3)\) per step in magnitude. Time to descend from \(\log n\) to 0:

$$\boxed{\ \mathbb{E}\,\sigma_\infty(n)\ \approx\ \frac{\log n}{\tfrac12\log(4/3)} = \frac{2}{\log(4/3)}\,\log n\ \approx\ 6.95212\,\log n\ }\qquad\text{(\(T\)-map steps, natural log).}$$

For the Syracuse map the per-step multiplier is \(3/2^{\nu_2(3n+1)}\) with \(\nu_2\) modelled as geometric, \(\Pr(\nu_2=j)=2^{-j}\), \(\mathbb{E}\,\nu_2 = 2\), giving the same drift in one line: \(\mathbb{E}[\log \tfrac{3}{2^{\nu_2}}] = \log 3 - 2\log 2 = \log\tfrac34 \approx -0.28768 < 0\).

Geometric-mean drift — try it. Over a long block, size is multiplied by roughly \(3^{k}/2^{k+\ell}\); with odd/even equally frequent this is \((3/4)^{k}\) per odd–even pair. For a general \(qx+1\) map the Syracuse drift is \(\log q - 2\log 2 = \log(q/4)\): negative for \(q=3\), positive for \(q=5\) — the heuristic reason \(3x+1\) should converge and \(5x+1\) should diverge (§8).


Section 6

Heuristic and probabilistic models

Everything in this section is HEURISTIC, except where a theorem about a model is stated.

  1. Crandall–Shanks / Terras coin model: parity vectors treated as i.i.d. fair coin flips. Predicts the drift and \(\mathbb{E}\sigma_\infty\) formulas of §5, Gaussian fluctuations of \(\sigma_\infty(n)\) around \(6.95212\log n\) with standard deviation \(\asymp\sqrt{\log n}\), and the average-order behaviour of \(\sigma_\infty\).
  2. Lagarias–Weiss (1992), two rigorous stochastic models: (i) a random walk modelling \(T \bmod 2^j\) (forward iteration), (ii) branching random walks modelling \(T^{-1} \bmod 3^j\) (backward iteration / the inverse tree). PROVEN about the models: both yield the same extremal constant, \(\limsup \sigma_\infty(n)/\log n = \gamma_0 \approx 41.677647\) — a large-deviation constant, not the typical value 6.95212. Confusing these two constants is a common error. Model predictions agreed with \(3x+1\) data up to \(10^{11}\) at the time.
  3. Kontorovich–Lagarias / Kontorovich–Sinai refinements: Syracuse random variables, 3-adic distributions of backward trees, Benford-type digit laws; the same framework applied to \(5x+1\) predicts the opposite behaviour (§8). arXiv:0910.1944
  4. Sinai's statistical \(3x+1\) model (2003) and later ergodic-theoretic models: treat the Syracuse map's invariant distributions directly.

Why convergence is expected

The drift is negative and not marginal in log scale (\(\log(3/4)\approx-0.29\) per Syracuse step); a random walk with negative drift hits any lower level with probability 1, and the large-deviation cost of staying high for \(k\) steps decays exponentially in \(k\). Combined with (a) density-1 descent (Terras–Everett, proved), (b) Tao's almost-boundedness (proved, log density), and (c) verification to \(10^{21}\), the heuristic picture is coherent and quantitatively accurate wherever it can be checked — stopping-time histograms, path-record growth \(\sim n^2\), \(\gamma\) records.

Where heuristics are known to be unreliable

  • The coin-flip assumption is false for individual orbits: parity vectors are deterministic, and §5 shows every pattern — including arbitrarily long all-odd runs — actually occurs for some \(n\) (e.g. \(n\equiv -1 \pmod{2^k}\) rises for \(k\) steps). The model describes typical \(n\); the conjecture is a worst-case (\(\forall n\)) statement. A density-1 conclusion can coexist with infinitely many exceptions — exactly what must be excluded.
  • The models are insensitive to the Diophantine obstructions that dominate cycles (§5): a random model “predicts” no cycles, but actual nonexistence for small \(m\) needs Baker theory, not probability.
  • Extremes are model-sensitive: predictions for \(\limsup \gamma(n)\), maximal excursions, and record holders come from large-deviation tails where different rigorous models must be checked against each other (Lagarias–Weiss did this; cruder models need not agree).
  • The \(5x+1\) warning cuts both ways: the same style of heuristic correctly predicts divergence there (§8), which supports the heuristic — but in the polynomial analogue \(\mathbb{F}_2[x]\) the analogous heuristics can be proved, and the integer case cannot, so “the heuristic is a proof in a neighbouring world” is not evidence transferable by logic.
  • Tao's theorem is the precise boundary of current rigour: the probabilistic idea can be made rigorous for almost all \(n\) (log density) and almost bounded values — and his paper explains why iterating the argument stalls before “all \(n\), value 1”.

Section 7

Hardness and undecidability

Conway's generalized Collatz functions (1972) PROVEN

Fix a modulus \(P\) and rationals \(a_i,b_i\) (\(0\le i<P\)) such that \(g(n)=a_i n + b_i\) is always an integer when \(n\equiv i\pmod P\). Ordinary Collatz is \(P=2\), \((a_0,b_0)=(1/2,0)\), \((a_1,b_1)=(3,1)\). Conway proved the problem “given \(g\) and \(n\), does the \(g\)-orbit of \(n\) reach 1?” is algorithmically undecidable, by encoding the halting problem / register machines in such a \(g\). J. H. Conway, Proc. 1972 Number Theory Conf., Boulder, 49–52; reprinted in The Ultimate Challenge (2010).

Kurtz–Simon (2007) PROVEN

The universally quantified version — “given \(g\), does every \(n>0\) reach 1 under \(g\)?” — is undecidable and in fact \(\Pi^0_2\)-complete in the arithmetical hierarchy. S. A. Kurtz & J. Simon, Proc. TAMC 2007, LNCS 4484, 542–553, DOI 10.1007/978-3-540-72504-6_49. (Secondary sources add that this holds even with the modulus fixed at \(P=6480\); that refinement was not re-verified from the primary text in the underlying research — the \(\Pi^0_2\)-completeness is primary-verified, the 6480 detail is secondary.)

FRACTRAN PROVEN

Conway's later formalism (1987): a FRACTRAN program is a finite ordered list of positive rationals \(f_1,\dots,f_k\); on input \(n\), replace \(n\) by \(n f_i\) for the first \(i\) with \(n f_i\in\mathbb{Z}\), and halt if none applies. FRACTRAN is Turing-universal (Conway's PRIMEGAME enumerates primes), and FRACTRAN programs are generalized Collatz functions in disguise — the applicable fraction is determined by \(n\) modulo the l.c.m. of denominators. This is the concrete bridge from Collatz-type iteration to universal computation. Conway, in Open Problems in Communication and Computation, Springer (1987), 4–26.

What this does NOT say PROVEN (logic) — the most misreported point in popular accounts. Undecidability of the uniform problem over all \(g\) implies nothing about the single instance \(g=C\): a specific \(\Pi^0_2\) sentence can be provable, disprovable, or independent, and no result cited here shows the Collatz conjecture is independent of PA or ZFC, or undecidable as a single statement. Any source claiming “Collatz is undecidable, therefore unprovable” is wrong. The correct moral is methodological: no uniform algorithm settling all generalized Collatz functions can exist, so a proof for \(3x+1\) must use the specific arithmetic of 3 and 2 — as the cycle results do.

Logical form. The conjecture is a \(\Pi^0_2\) sentence \(\forall n\,\exists k\; C^k(n)=1\) with decidable matrix — hence if it is false, a nontrivial cycle would be a finite, checkable counterexample; whereas divergence of a specific orbit is not finitely verifiable in general.

Section 8

Generalizations and variants

(a) \(3x+d\) maps

For odd \(d\), \(C_d(n)=n/2\) (\(n\) even), \(3n+d\) (\(n\) odd). PROVEN (elementary): rescaling relates \(3x+d\) on multiples of \(d\) to \(3x+1\) — if \(n=d\,m\) then \(3n+d = d(3m+1)\) — so cycles / divergence for \(3x+1\) embed into \(3x+d\); new behaviour occurs on other residue classes. Known cycle counts for small \(d\) are computational tabulations (Matthews). Statements like “\(3x+d\) always cycles” are CONJECTURAL in general (Matthews–Watts conjectures, below), not theorems. Source: Matthews, “Generalized \(3x+1\) mappings” (PDF).

(b) \(qx+1\), especially \(5x+1\)

Drift per Syracuse step is \(\log(q/4)\) (§5): for \(q=5\), \(\log(5/4)>0\).

  • It is CONJECTURAL that a density-1 set of \(5x+1\) orbits diverge to \(\infty\), and that only finitely many cycles exist — the positive ones (for \(C_5\)) being \(\{1,3,8,4,2\}\) and \(\{13,33,83,208,104,52,26\}\), plus \(\{-1,-2\}\) on negatives; the sets entering these cycles are conjectured to have density 0.
  • No divergent \(5x+1\) orbit has been proved divergent: “\(n=7\) appears divergent” is COMPUTATIONAL observation (its orbit grows beyond all computed bounds), not a proof. In \(T_5\) form the apparent cycles start at \(0,1,13,17,-1\) and the trajectory from 7 appears divergent — the two cycle lists describe the same cycles in \(C\) vs \(T\) coordinates; check the map before comparing lists.
  • For \(qx+1\) with \(q\ge5\) odd, the stochastic models are rigorously analysable and predict divergence for almost all orbits; transferring “almost all diverge” to the actual \(5x+1\) map is CONJECTURAL. Sources: Kontorovich–Lagarias, arXiv:0910.1944, §1.2, §7; Matthews (above).

(c) Collatz over the negative integers

PROVEN cycles; completeness CONJECTURAL. Extending \(C\) to \(\mathbb{Z}_{<0}\) with the same formula, three nontrivial cycles are known (plus \(\{0\}\) and the positive \((1,4,2)\)):

$$-1 \to -2 \to -1 \quad(\text{length }2),$$ $$-5 \to -14 \to -7 \to -20 \to -10 \to -5 \quad(\text{length }5),$$

−17 → −50 → −25 → −74 → −37 → −110 → −55 → −164 → −82 → −41 → −122 → −61 → −182 → −91 → −272 → −136 → −68 → −34 → −17  (length 18)

In \(T\) form these are \(\{-1\}\), \(\{-5,-7,-10\}\), \(\{-17,-25,-37,-55,-82,-41,-61,-91,-136,-68,-34\}\). It is conjectured — not proved — that every negative integer reaches one of these three, with no other negative cycles or divergent negative orbits. Positive and negative orbits never cross (\(C\) preserves sign for \(n\ne0\)) PROVEN (elementary). Sources: Matthews, slides (PDF); Kontorovich–Lagarias, arXiv:0910.1944.

(d) Generalized Collatz mappings (Matthews–Watts)

\(T_{d,\mathbf{m},\mathbf{r}}(x)=(m_i x + r_i)/d\) for \(x\equiv i\pmod d\), with integrality conditions. CONJECTURAL (Matthews–Watts / relatively-prime conjecture): if the \(m_i\) are coprime to \(d\) and \(|m_0 m_1\cdots m_{d-1}| < d^{d}\), all trajectories eventually cycle; if \(|m_0\cdots m_{d-1}| > d^{d}\), almost all trajectories diverge; in either case there are finitely many cycles. The \(3x+1\) case is \(1\cdot3 < 2^2\); \(5x+1\) is \(1\cdot5 > 2^2\) — one inequality explaining both. Partial density results toward this are PROVEN (finite stopping time has density 1 in the \(<d^d\) relatively-prime case, generalizing Terras).

(e) Other domains

  • Rationals with odd denominator (\(\mathbb{Q}_{(2)}\)): cycles correspond to 2-adic periodic points; Lagarias proved the set of rational cycles is finite-type / classified in part (Acta Arith. 56 (1990), 33–53). Individual listed rational cycles are PROVEN by direct verification; completeness is CONJECTURAL (Finite Cycles Conjecture: only finitely many cycles in \(\mathbb{Q}_{(2)}\)).
  • \(\mathbb{F}_2[x]\) analogue: \(T_1(f)=f/x\) if \(x\mid f\), \(((x+1)f+1)/x\) otherwise. Here the analogue is PROVEN: all orbits reach 1, \(\tau_1(f)\in O(\deg(f)^{1.5})\), and average stopping time \(\rho_1(n)/n\to2\) (Inselmann 2024/2025, confirming Alon–Behajaina–Paran). arXiv:2401.12781. The clearest case of “no established integer result — but a complete result in the function-field analogue, because carries do not exist there”.
  • Maps where the naive picture fails: higher-dimensional and number-field generalized Collatz maps where divergent cones of positive density are PROVEN (Kionke, arXiv:1511.05893) — the Matthews–Watts picture fails verbatim over general number fields \(K\ne\mathbb{Q}\). A genuine negative result for naive generalization, not evidence about \(\mathbb{Z}\).

Section 9

Connections

  • Dynamical systems. \(T\) on \(\mathbb{Z}_2\) is a paradigmatic non-uniformly expanding, measure-preserving system conjugate to a shift (§5); predecessor trees, symbolic dynamics (parity sequences as itineraries), and transfer operators are the working tools. Wirsching's monograph is built entirely on predecessor sets. Wirsching (1998), Springer
  • Ergodic theory. PROVEN \(T\) preserves Haar measure on \(\mathbb{Z}_2\) and is ergodic, indeed strongly mixing (Matthews–Watts theorem as stated by Lagarias); the conjugacy \(\Phi\) is measure-preserving. Hence Haar-a.e. \(x\in\mathbb{Z}_2\) has equidistributed orbit statistics — while \(\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\) is null. Akin's “Why is the \(3x+1\) problem hard?” analyses exactly this tension.
  • 2-adic analysis. §5 is the connection: \(\Phi\), its fixed points (Bernstein–Lagarias conjecture: the only odd fixed points of \(\Phi\) are \(-1\) and \(1/3\) — CONJECTURAL), solenoidal maps, and 2-adic equidistribution are the engine inside Tao's proof — via Syracuse random variables and 3-adic fine-scale mixing (note Tao mixes 2-adic input distribution with 3-adic output equidistribution).
  • Computability theory. §7: Conway / Kurtz–Simon / FRACTRAN; also Collatz-like termination encodings in rewriting systems and the Busy-Beaver / “cryptid” phenomenon for small Turing machines whose halting reduces to Collatz-like iteration — cited here only as a connection, not as a verified specific machine claim (see caveats).
  • Diophantine equations / transcendence. Cycle exclusion is Diophantine approximation: \(|N\log2 - k\log3|\) small (continued fractions, §5) + Baker–Matveev lower bounds for linear forms in logarithms + the exponential Diophantine equation \(2^N-3^k = (\text{small / controlled})\). Pillai-type problems are neighbouring open territory; do not claim Collatz is equivalent to Pillai or \(abc\) — no such equivalence is proved.
  • Probability / stochastic processes. §6: random walks, branching random walks, large deviations (Lagarias–Weiss), Gaussian fluctuations of stopping times.
  • Analytic number theory. Density theorems (§4a), counting via difference inequalities / linear programming (§4b), 3-adic predecessor densities (Wirsching), Benford's law for \(3x+1\) orbits (Lagarias–Soundararajan, J. London Math. Soc. 74 (2006), 289–303, DOI 10.1112/S0024610706023131 — bibliographic detail secondary-verified).

Section 10

Open problems and current frontier

What specifically remains unproven CONJECTURAL

  1. Exclude a divergent positive orbit. No orbit is known to diverge; no proof bounds all orbits.
  2. Exclude a nontrivial positive cycle with \(m\ge92\) circuits — push §4c from bounded \(m\) to all \(m\). Current methods give, for each fixed \(m\), exclusion for small \(m\) with bounds exponential in \(m\) — no uniform argument.
  3. Improve Tao from logarithmic density + \(f(N)\) to natural density + constant, or to all \(N\). Each step is a qualitative jump, not a refinement.
  4. Improve Krasikov–Lagarias \(x^{0.84}\) to \(x^{1-o(1)}\) or positive density \(\pi_1(x)\gg x\) (Wirsching's positive-predecessor-density programme aims at the latter; open).
  5. Named conjectures, all CONJECTURAL: Terras's Coefficient Stopping Time Conjecture (§5); the Bernstein–Lagarias \(\Phi\) Fixed Point Conjecture (§9); the Finite Cycles Conjecture in \(\mathbb{Q}_{(2)}\) (§8e); the Matthews–Watts conjectures (§8d).
  6. No established results — here's why: there is no known upper bound on \(\sigma_\infty(n)\) valid for all \(n\), and no known nontrivial lower bound on \(\mathrm{Col}_{\min}(N)\) for all \(N\) beyond trivialities — because either would interact directly with (1)–(2). This is a genuine gap, not an omission in this brief.

Finite verification + density theorems + heuristics, in any combination, do not suffice.
